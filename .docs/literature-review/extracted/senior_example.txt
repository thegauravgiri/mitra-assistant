HEAR MY HANDS
A Python-Based Real-Time Sign Language Interpreter Using
Computer Vision and Machine Learning

Biplav Uprety || Sneha Neupane || Sushan Rana Magar || Rimisha Khadka

Introduction
Hear My Hands is designed to use a webcam or mobile camera to recognize at least 50 common
signs and convert them into text and speech in real time. The project aims for 85% accuracy and
a response time of less than four seconds. The system is based on Python, OpenCV, Media Pipe,
and CNNs, and it follows a workflow from camera input to sign recognition output.
Researchers do not treat sign language recognition as a simple gesture classification problem.
This is because sign languages use both manual features, such as hand shape and movement, and
non-manual features, such as facial expressions and body posture. In addition, successful sign
language systems need to consider language, Deaf culture, accessibility, and human-computer
interaction, not just computer vision techniques (Bragg et al., 2019; Liang et al., 2023; Rastgoo
et al., 2021). Research on Turkish sign language also shows that signs involve hand orientation,
movement, posture, and facial information at the same time. This means that systems that only
look at cropped hand images may miss important information (Sincan & Keles, 2020).

Theoretical Background
Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning
While machine learning (ML) enables systems to recognize patterns in data, artificial
intelligence (AI) enables systems to mimic human intelligence. In order to correctly classify
and interpret signs, machine learning models are trained using datasets of hand gestures.

Computer Vision
In order to capture and process visual input from cameras, computer vision is essential.
Real-time hand shape, movement, and position identification is achieved through methods
like image processing, object detection, and feature extraction.

Deep Learning Techniques
Gesture recognition makes extensive use of deep learning models like Convolutional
Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). While RNNs are helpful
for sequential gesture interpretation, CNNs aid in the recognition of spatial features, such
as hand shapes.

Critical Analysis and Synthesis
An important idea in the literature is the difference between isolated sign recognition,
continuous sign recognition, and sign language translation. Isolated sign recognition
identifies one sign from one video clip. Continuous sign recognition identifies a sequence
of signs over time. Sign language translation goes one step further by converting signs into
spoken-language sentences, where the grammar and word order may be different from the
original sign language (Liang et al., 2023; Camgoz et al., 2018; Camgoz et al., 2020). Since

Hear My Hands focuses on 50 common signs and is measured mainly by accuracy and
speed, it is more suitable as an isolated sign recognition system rather than a full
translation system, even though it produces text and speech output (Liang et al., 2023;
Camgoz et al., 2020). Previous studies on large continuous sign language datasets, such as
RWTH-PHOENIX-Weather, show that continuous recognition requires much larger labelled
datasets and more complex temporal models than a short project usually allows (Forster et
al., 2014; Cui et al., 2019).

Advancements in Sign Language Recognition
Another major trend in the literature is the shift from special sensors to regular RGB
camera systems. For example, BosphorusSign was created using Kinect v2 depth sensing,
and AUTSL includes RGB, depth, and skeleton data. On the other hand, MS-ASL and WLASL
show that large real-world RGB datasets can support signer-independent recognition using
more practical and accessible hardware (Camgoz et al., 2016; Sincan & Keles, 2020; Vaezi
Joze & Koller, 2018; Li et al., 2020). This supports the decision in Hear My Hands to use a
webcam or mobile camera. However, it also means the system must deal with more
variation in lighting, background, camera angle, and signing style compared with systems
built in controlled environments (Vaezi Joze & Koller, 2018; Sincan & Keles, 2020; Li et al.,
2020).
The development of sign language models has also changed over time. Earlier systems
often used handcrafted features, but recent work has moved toward deep learning

methods that can learn both spatial and temporal patterns. These include recurrent
models for continuous recognition and transformer-based models for recognition and
translation, because many signs depend on movement over time and not just on a single
hand shape (Cui et al., 2019; Camgoz et al., 2020; Rastgoo et al., 2021). For a low-cost realtime project, landmark-based methods are still useful. MediaPipe Hands can track hand
landmarks in real time from a single RGB camera, which reduces the amount of work
needed for the recognition model (Zhang et al., 2020). For this reason, the use of Python,
OpenCV, MediaPipe, webcam input, feature extraction, and a CNN-based recognition stage
in Hear My Hands is in line with current practice. At the same time, the literature suggests
that adding temporal models and including body or face information could improve the
system beyond simple frame-by-frame classification (Zhang et al., 2020; Sincan & Keles,
2020; Rastgoo et al., 2021).
Another common finding in the literature is that the quality and diversity of data often affect
performance more than the choice of model itself, especially when systems are tested on
new users and in realistic situations (Vaezi Joze & Koller, 2018; Li et al., 2020; Sincan &
Keles, 2020). Our project also identifies low accuracy, limited training data, and camera
limitations as key risks. This suggests that collecting data from multiple signers, keeping
the classes balanced, and testing the system in realistic conditions should be major
priorities. The literature therefore supports building a local dataset for the selected 50 signs
with variation in clothing, lighting, camera angle, background, and signer identity if the
project wants to achieve 85% accuracy outside a controlled lab setting (Vaezi Joze & Koller,
2018; Li et al., 2020; Sincan & Keles, 2020).

The literature also makes it clear that sign languages are not universal. Different public
datasets are usually built for specific sign languages, such as American Sign Language,
German Sign Language, and Turkish Sign Language, rather than for a single global sign
language system (Bragg et al., 2019; Forster et al., 2014; Sincan & Keles, 2020; Li et al.,
2020). We will be using the Australian Sign Language vocabulary. This is important because
the vocabulary, annotations, and benchmark comparisons all depend on the chosen sign
language. Domain-specific datasets such as BosphorusSign also show that a limited
vocabulary in areas such as health, finance, or daily communication can be a practical
starting point for assistive technology, which fits well with the project’s proposed use in
community, education, and healthcare settings (Camgoz et al., 2016).

Conclusion
Overall, the literature suggests that Hear My Hands is a realistic first-stage assistive
technology project if it is treated as a vision-based isolated sign recognition system that
uses standard RGB cameras, real-time hand landmark extraction, and a lightweight
temporal classifier to produce text and speech for a clearly defined vocabulary and target
sign language (Liang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020). This design fits the
project’s low-cost hardware plan, 13-week development timeline, and clear performance
targets. It also provides a strong foundation for future work on larger vocabularies,
continuous recognition, and full sign language translation when more time and more
diverse data become available (Camgoz et al., 2020; Forster et al., 2014).
